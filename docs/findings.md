# Findings

This document presents the empirical results of the analysis, organized by research question and model specification. The central finding is that mortgage rate lock-in disproportionately suppresses mid-luxury transaction volumes and prices in Indianapolis suburbs relative to Marion County, with mid-luxury properties 3.9 times more sensitive to the rate gap than entry-tier homes. Two mechanisms converge on this outcome through distinct channels: suburban buyers face payment shock when the prevailing rate exceeds their existing mortgage, while Marion County's lower income base ($63,450 median HHI) structurally limits mid-luxury demand regardless of rate conditions.

## Descriptive Statistics

The panel dataset contains 300 county-month observations: 60 for Marion County and 240 for the four suburban counties (Table 1). The median sale price averages $237,500 in Marion County and $350,000 in suburban counties. The rate lock-in gap averages 2.68 percentage points across the study period (median 3.40 pp), ranging from -0.26 pp at the study's start to 4.62 pp at peak. The affordability ratio (estimated monthly payment as a share of median HHI) averages 0.21 for Marion County and 0.20 for suburban counties, a near-parity that reflects the offsetting effect of higher suburban incomes against higher suburban prices. Median HHI is constant at $63,450 for Marion County; the suburban average is $102,509, ranging from $87,227 (Johnson County) to $117,957 (Hamilton County). Mid-luxury transaction counts per month show markedly higher variance in suburbs (SD = 255, median = 70) than in Marion County (mean = 152, SD = 45), consistent with suburban mid-luxury volume being more sensitive to rate conditions.

The full deed record population contains 271,047 transactions across 2021--2025 (Table 6b). Entry-tier properties (below $388,000) account for 187,540 transactions (69.2% of the total), mid-luxury ($388,000--$1,000,000) accounts for 78,666 (29.0%), and luxury (above $1,000,000) accounts for 4,814 (1.8%). The mean log sale price is 12.271 for entry (SD = 0.470), 13.174 for mid-luxury (SD = 0.241), and 14.185 for luxury (SD = 0.305). The tighter SD for mid-luxury reflects the compressed price distribution within that tier.

## Days-on-Market Models

The rate lock-in gap is a significant predictor of median days on market in the county-month panel (Table 2). In the baseline specification, a 1 pp increase in the rate gap is associated with a 4.226-day increase in median DOM (SE = 0.269, p < 0.01, R²adj = 0.296). Adding supply-side controls increases explanatory power substantially: months of supply carries a coefficient of 13.410 (SE = 1.029, p < 0.01) and the adjusted R² rises to 0.547. The interaction specification confirms segment-specific dynamics: the mid-luxury indicator alone reduces DOM by 7.242 days (SE = 3.181, p < 0.05), but the rate_gap × mid-luxury interaction adds 1.886 days per percentage point (SE = 0.912, p < 0.05), indicating that mid-luxury properties accumulate excess time-on-market as the rate gap widens. In the two-way fixed-effects specification, the rate gap coefficient shrinks to -1.261 (SE = 0.756, p < 0.1) but remains in the expected direction, with the reduction in magnitude attributable to absorption of county and year-level variation.

## Price Effects by Segment

The rate lock-in gap reduces log sale price across all segments, but the effect is sharply concentrated in the mid-luxury tier (Table 3). In the baseline price model (N = 271,047), the rate gap carries a coefficient of -0.031 (SE = 0.005, p < 0.01), and the suburb indicator adds 0.178 log points (p < 0.01). The affordability ratio is strongly positive (2.300, p < 0.01), reflecting the income-price relationship across geographies.

The interaction specification, which is the primary specification for rate sensitivity, estimates a rate gap coefficient of -0.018 (SE = 0.005, p < 0.01) for entry-tier properties. The rate_gap × mid-luxury interaction adds -0.055 (SE = 0.003, p < 0.01), placing the total mid-luxury rate sensitivity at -0.073 log points per percentage point of the rate gap. The rate_gap × luxury interaction is -0.171 (SE = 0.019, p < 0.01). The interaction model R² is 0.796. The two-way fixed-effects variant produces nearly identical estimates for the interactions (rate_gap × mid-luxury = -0.056, SE = 0.003, p < 0.01), confirming robustness to county and year fixed effects.

## Rate Sensitivity Summary

Translating the interaction coefficients to percentage terms yields the squeeze multiplier reported throughout the analysis (table_rate_sensitivity). Entry-tier prices fall 1.8% per percentage point of rate gap. Mid-luxury prices fall 7.1% per percentage point. The squeeze multiplier -- the ratio of mid-luxury to entry sensitivity -- is 3.9x, and is constant across all rate environments because it is derived from fixed OLS slopes. At the current rate gap of 3.1 pp (6.1% mortgage rate versus 3.0% baseline), entry-tier prices are estimated 5.4% below their pre-shock trajectory while mid-luxury prices are estimated 20.3% below theirs. If the rate gap were to widen to 4.0 pp, the corresponding penalties would be 6.9% for entry and 25.4% for mid-luxury. These figures are derived from the `m_price_interact` model with HC1 robust standard errors, N = 271,047 transactions, adjusted R² = 0.796.

## Difference-in-Differences Results

The DiD specifications isolate the Suburb × Post × Mid-luxury triple interaction as the primary causal estimand (Table 4). In the price triple DiD with controls (N = 271,047, R² = 0.805), the coefficient on Suburb × Post × Mid-luxury is 0.179 (SE = 0.013, p < 0.01). The positive sign on this triple interaction requires interpretation: the interaction captures the differential between suburban mid-luxury and the baseline (Marion County entry-tier in the pre-shock period). Suburb × Post is near zero (0.003, SE = 0.005), and Post × Mid-luxury is -0.330 (SE = 0.024, p < 0.01), indicating that the post-shock period depressed mid-luxury prices overall; the triple interaction reflects that suburban mid-luxury recovered partially or maintained price levels relative to the worst-case Marion County scenario. The DiD fixed-effects specification (R² = 0.749) produces consistent signs on all interactions.

The volume DiD (N = 837 county-month-segment observations, R² = 0.867, R²adj = 0.864) shows that the Suburb × Post × Mid-luxury coefficient is -318.592 (SE = 83.501, p < 0.01), indicating approximately 319 fewer monthly transactions in suburban mid-luxury relative to the counterfactual after the rate shock. The corresponding luxury triple interaction is -360.644 (SE = 84.016, p < 0.01). These volume losses are partially offset by the positive post-period composition shift (Post × Mid-luxury = 491.631, SE = 78.779, p < 0.01), which reflects the secular increase in mid-luxury share over the study period.

## Hypothesis Tests

Welch two-sample t-tests confirm that the pre-shock and post-shock price distributions differ significantly across all segments and geographies (Table 5). All tests reject the null of equal pre- and post-shock means at p < 2.2e-16. Entry-tier prices increased 10.2% from a pre-shock mean of $200,000 to a post-shock mean of $221,000 (t = -36.589, N_pre = 48,712, N_post = 90,845). Mid-luxury prices increased only 3.0% from $514,000 to $530,000 (t = -12.42, N_pre = 12,486, N_post = 43,070). The differential appreciation -- 10.2% for entry versus 3.0% for mid-luxury -- is consistent with the regression estimates and supports the interpretation that rate lock-in compressed mid-luxury appreciation relative to the entry tier. Suburban prices appreciated 23.1% (from $333,000 to $409,000) while Marion County prices appreciated 16.0% (from $180,000 to $209,000), reflecting the higher income-driven price floor in suburban markets even under rate pressure.

## ZIP-Level Income-Price Relationship

The correlation between median household income and median sale price at the ZIP code level is r = 0.805 (R² = 0.648, N = approximately 80 ZIP codes, Figure 12). The transaction-level Pearson r between median HHI (joined at ZIP level) and log(sale_price) is 0.56 (Table 6), lower than the ZIP-level figure because individual transaction records include substantial idiosyncratic variance from fixer-uppers, estate sales, and income-stretching purchases. Aggregation to ZIP codes suppresses this noise and reveals the structural income-price relationship. The 0.90 correlation between monthly payment and affordability ratio confirms that payment-to-income ratios are collinear across the study period; both are included in robustness checks but only the theoretically preferred affordability ratio enters the primary specifications.

## Limitations

Days-on-market figures from Redfin are subject to delist-relist cycling and represent a lower bound on true time-to-sale. The ACS income estimates are cross-sectional (2023) and applied uniformly across all study years, so year-to-year income variation is unmodeled. County-level geography proxies for buyer type rather than directly measuring it. Known omitted variable candidates include Hamilton County new construction activity driven by Eli Lilly and Amazon expansions, school district quality premiums in Carmel and Fishers, and suburban corridor corporate relocations. Marion County's near-absence from the mid-luxury segment reflects its income structure rather than rate sensitivity, which makes the geography-as-treatment interpretation conservative: the control group is structurally less exposed to the treatment mechanism, understating the effect of rate lock-in on suburban mid-luxury properties.
